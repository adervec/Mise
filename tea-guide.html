01

Origins & Culture

Tea is the most-consumed drink on earth after water, yet nearly all of it springs from a single evergreen shrub. From a mythic Chinese emperor's accidental cup to the meditative silence of the Japanese tea room and the milky comfort of a British afternoon, every tradition is a different answer to the same leaf.

2737 BCE · CHINA (LEGEND)

Legend credits Emperor Shennong with discovering tea when leaves blew into his boiling water. Whether myth or memory, it places tea's roots deep in ancient China as medicine before pleasure.

618–907 CE · TANG DYNASTY

Lu Yu's Cha Jing ("The Classic of Tea") codified cultivation and brewing, elevating tea from tonic to art and philosophy across the Chinese empire.

9TH C. · JAPAN

Buddhist monks carried tea seeds and ritual from China. Centuries later, Sen no Rikyū refined the chanoyu ceremony around wabi-sabi — harmony, respect, purity, and tranquility.

1610 · THE NETHERLANDS

Dutch traders brought the first commercial tea to Europe, where it became an exotic luxury for the wealthy before spreading outward.

1773 · BOSTON

The Boston Tea Party turned a chest of tea into a symbol of revolt, binding the leaf to the politics of empire and independence.

1840s · INDIA

The British established vast plantations in Assam and Darjeeling to break China's monopoly — creating the robust black teas that define the Western cup.

NOW · GLOBAL REVIVAL

Gongfu brewing, single-estate sourcing, and specialty culture have renewed reverence for terroir, harvest, and craft — tea's own "third wave."

Tea is naught but this: heat water, prepare tea, and drink with attention.
02

One Plant, Six Colors

Green, white, oolong, black, yellow, and dark (pu-erh) tea all come from Camellia sinensis. What separates them is not the plant but processing — chiefly how much the picked leaf is allowed to oxidize before heat halts the reaction.

When a leaf is bruised and exposed to air, enzymes turn pale catechins into darker theaflavins and thearubigins — the same browning you see in a cut apple. Stop it early and the cup stays green and grassy; let it run to completion and it turns dark, malty, and robust. Herbal "teas" (tisanes) contain no Camellia at all.

03

The Science of Steeping

Steeping is selective extraction. Hot water pulls flavor, aroma, color, and chemistry from the leaf in a deliberate order — and the steeper's whole job is to stop at the point of balance.

What comes out, and when

Three families of compounds dominate. Caffeine extracts quickly and brings stimulation and a clean bitterness. Polyphenols (catechins and tannins) bring astringency, structure, and antioxidant character — they extract more slowly and intensify with time and heat, which is why an over-steeped cup turns harsh and puckering. L-theanine, an amino acid abundant in shaded teas, lends sweetness, umami, and a calm, focused alertness that softens caffeine's edge. Volatile aromatics — hundreds of them — give each tea its scent and fade fastest.

🌡️

Temperature

The master dial. Delicate green and white want 70–80°C; robust black and pu-erh want near-boiling. Too hot scalds delicate leaves into bitterness.

⏱️

Time

Astringent tannins build with every second. Greens may need only 1–2 min; blacks 3–5. Past the window, balance tips to bitter.

🍃

Leaf Quality

Whole leaves extract gently and evenly; broken "dust" in bags extracts fast and harsh. Surface area is everything.

💧

Water

Soft, fresh, well-oxygenated water near neutral pH. Over-boiled or hard water flattens aroma and dulls flavor.

⚖️

Leaf-to-Water

Roughly 2–3g per 200ml for Western brewing; far more leaf and far shorter steeps for gongfu style.

♻️

Re-steeping

Quality whole-leaf teas yield several infusions, each revealing a new layer. The first steep is rarely the best.

The caffeine myth

Color does not predict strength: a pale white tea can carry as much caffeine as a dark black one, since content depends on the leaf, bud ratio, and steep — not oxidation. And the old advice to "pour off the first steep to remove caffeine" is largely a myth; only a modest fraction leaves in the opening seconds.

05–11

The Styles

Seven ways to meet the leaf. Each balances freshness against depth, simplicity against ceremony, delicacy against strength.

Unoxidized · Heat-Fixed

Green Tea

70–80°C1–3 min2–3g / 200mlFresh & grassy

The least processed true tea, fixed with heat almost immediately after picking to halt oxidation. Chinese greens are pan-fired (nutty, toasty); Japanese greens are steamed (marine, vegetal, umami). Fresh, delicate, and the most temperature-sensitive of all — the cup most easily ruined by water that's too hot.

  1. Heat water and let it cool to 70–80°C — boiling water scalds green leaves into bitterness.
  2. Warm the teapot or gaiwan, then add ~2–3g of leaf per 200ml.
  3. Pour water gently down the side, not directly onto the leaves.
  4. Steep just 1–2 minutes for the first infusion; taste and stop early.
  5. Decant fully, then re-steep 2–3 times with slightly hotter water and longer times.
Strengths
  • Fresh, light, high in antioxidants and L-theanine
  • Calm, focused energy; low astringency done right
  • Several delicate re-steeps
  • Lower caffeine than black, generally
Tradeoffs
  • Very unforgiving of over-hot water
  • Turns bitter and grassy if over-steeped
  • Short shelf life; fades quickly when stale
  • Subtle flavors lost on robust palates
Fully Oxidized · Bold

Black Tea

95–100°C3–5 min2–3g / 200mlMalty & robust

Fully oxidized, giving the dark, malty, robust cup that dominates the West. From brisk Assam and floral Darjeeling to smoky Lapsang and the blended backbone of English Breakfast, it stands up to milk and sugar and survives near-boiling water with ease — the most forgiving true tea.

  1. Bring fresh water to a full boil (95–100°C).
  2. Warm the pot, then add ~2–3g of leaf per 200ml.
  3. Pour water directly over the leaves and cover.
  4. Steep 3–5 minutes — longer for strength, but tannins turn harsh past the window.
  5. Strain fully; add milk or sugar to taste if desired. Whole-leaf grades give 1–2 good re-steeps.
Strengths
  • Bold, full-bodied, satisfying
  • Forgiving — hard to ruin with hot water
  • Pairs with milk, sugar, and food
  • Long shelf life; highest caffeine of the styles
Tradeoffs
  • Astringent and bitter if over-steeped
  • Fewer re-steeps than greens or oolongs
  • Can overwhelm subtle palates
  • Higher caffeine may disrupt sleep
Partially Oxidized · Gongfu

Oolong

85–95°C2–4 min3–5g / 200mlComplex

The connoisseur's category, oxidized anywhere from 10% to 80% and often rolled and roasted. The result spans light, floral, green-leaning teas to dark, toasty, honeyed ones. The pride of Fujian and Taiwan, it's the classic subject of gongfu brewing — many tiny, intense, evolving steeps from one pot of leaf.

  1. Heat water to 85–95°C depending on oxidation (lighter = cooler).
  2. Use a generous leaf dose in a small gaiwan or clay pot — gongfu uses far more leaf, far less water.
  3. Rinse the leaves with a quick pour and discard to awaken them.
  4. Steep the first infusion 20–40s (gongfu) or 2–3 min (Western); decant completely.
  5. Re-steep many times, adding a few seconds each round — flavor evolves across 4–8 infusions.
Strengths
  • Extraordinary complexity and range
  • Many evolving re-steeps from one dose
  • Balanced body — between green and black
  • Rewards attention and ritual
Tradeoffs
  • Gongfu method has a learning curve
  • Quality leaf is expensive
  • Needs more gear (gaiwan, small cups)
  • Temperature varies by sub-type
Minimally Processed · Delicate

White Tea

75–85°C3–5 min2–3g / 200mlSubtle

The gentlest processing of all: young buds and leaves simply withered and dried, barely touched. The cup is pale, delicate, and naturally sweet, with notes of hay, melon, and honey. Prized varieties like Silver Needle (Bai Hao Yin Zhen) come from Fujian's downy spring buds and reward a patient, low-heat steep.

  1. Heat water to 75–85°C; the delicate buds dislike boiling water.
  2. Use a slightly higher leaf dose, as fluffy buds take up volume.
  3. Pour gently and steep longer than green — 3–5 minutes — to coax out the subtle sweetness.
  4. Taste as it develops; white tea is forgiving of longer steeps but not of high heat.
  5. Re-steep 2–3 times, extending the time with each round.
Strengths
  • Delicate, naturally sweet, smooth
  • Very low astringency
  • Minimal processing; high antioxidants
  • Ages well — some improve over years
Tradeoffs
  • Subtle to the point of faint for some
  • Among the most expensive by weight
  • Easy to under-extract and miss
  • Quality varies enormously
Fermented · Aged

Pu-erh (Dark Tea)

95–100°C10–30s gongfu4–5g / 200mlEarthy

The only true tea that is microbially fermented and aged, sometimes for decades, from the broad-leaf trees of Yunnan, China. "Raw" (sheng) ages slowly toward mellow complexity; "ripe" (shou) is fast-fermented for a smooth, earthy, woodsy depth. Pressed into cakes and traded like wine, it is the deep end of the tea world.

  1. Break a portion from the cake and use ~4–5g per 200ml.
  2. Bring water to a full boil — pu-erh wants the hottest water of all.
  3. Rinse the leaves once (or twice for aged cakes) and discard to wake and clean them.
  4. Steep very briefly gongfu-style (10–30s), decanting fully each time.
  5. Re-steep 8–15+ times, lengthening gradually as the deep flavors unfold.
Strengths
  • Deep, smooth, earthy, warming
  • Many, many re-steeps from one dose
  • Often gentle on the stomach; aids digestion
  • Ages and appreciates like fine wine
Tradeoffs
  • Earthy/musty notes are an acquired taste
  • Quality and authenticity hard to judge
  • Good aged cakes are costly
  • Best results need gongfu technique
Whisked Powder · Ceremony

Matcha

70–80°Cwhisk, no steep~2g powderUmami

Not steeped but suspended: shade-grown green leaves are stone-ground into a fine powder and whisked directly into water, so you drink the whole leaf. Central to the Japanese tea ceremony, the shading boosts chlorophyll and L-theanine for a vivid green color, rich umami, and a uniquely calm, sustained alertness.

  1. Sift ~2g (about 1–2 tsp) of matcha into a warmed bowl to remove clumps.
  2. Add a small amount of 70–80°C water (never boiling).
  3. Whisk briskly in a "W" or "M" motion with a bamboo chasen until a fine, even froth forms.
  4. For usucha (thin), add more water; for koicha (thick), use less and fold gently.
  5. Drink promptly, before the powder settles.
Strengths
  • Whole-leaf — maximal antioxidants and L-theanine
  • Smooth, sustained, jitter-free energy
  • Rich umami and vivid color
  • Versatile in lattes and cooking
Tradeoffs
  • Needs special tools (whisk, sifter, bowl)
  • Ceremonial-grade is expensive
  • Clumps and turns bitter if mishandled
  • Strong, vegetal taste isn't for everyone
Tisane · No Camellia

Herbal Infusions

95–100°C5–7 min2–3g / 200mlCaffeine-free

Technically not tea at all but tisanes — infusions of herbs, flowers, roots, fruit, and spices, with no Camellia sinensis. Chamomile soothes, peppermint refreshes, rooibos offers a naturally caffeine-free body, hibiscus brings tart ruby color, and ginger warms. Forgiving, caffeine-free, and steeped long and hot.

  1. Bring water to a full boil — most herbs and roots need the heat.
  2. Use a generous amount of dried botanicals (they're light and bulky).
  3. Steep long, 5–7 minutes or more — tisanes rarely turn bitter.
  4. Cover while steeping to trap volatile aromatic oils, especially with mint.
  5. Strain and sweeten if desired; many are excellent iced.
Strengths
  • Caffeine-free; great in the evening
  • Very forgiving — hard to over-steep
  • Huge variety of flavors and benefits
  • Often soothing or functional
Tradeoffs
  • No theanine; different chemistry from true tea
  • Some herbs interact with medications
  • Quality and potency vary widely
  • Few re-steeps; flavor spends in one go
"Tea is a way of life as much as a drink."

Start with the right temperature for your leaf. Steep, taste, and steep again — the second and third infusions often hold the best of the cup.
